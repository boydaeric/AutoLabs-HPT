*THEIA Services · Public note · September 6, 2026; revised [DATE]*

# Executive summary

Under CMS’s central estimates, two proposed rules would leave providers about $488 billion to $753 billion worse off over 2026–2035, in 2026 dollars, net of lower provider taxes and, at the lower end, of the most that providers could stop paying to fund the cut payments. [I] The rules would carry out two sections of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA): Section 71115 limits the taxes states levy on providers to fund Medicaid, and Section 71116 caps state directed payments (SDPs), extra payments a state requires its Medicaid managed care plans to make to particular providers (sec. 1). [E] CMS measures its SDP and provider tax estimates against a projection of Medicaid without the OBBBA (sec. 3) [E], so they include the OBBBA’s own effect. [I] CMS publishes no hospital figure: it expects most of the SDP rule’s reductions to fall on hospitals but says it lacks the data to attribute them by provider type (sec. 3). [E]

THEIA Services found no published estimate of this combined net loss. [I] The outside estimates this note examines, from the Healthcare Financial Management Association (HFMA), KFF and POLITICO, each measure something narrower or on a different basis, and CMS’s own figures and an $891 billion HHS estimate measure different things again (sec. 5). [I] CMS itself says the two rules together would on net still cut provider payments significantly over time (sec. 4) [E], without giving a combined figure. [I]

The $753 billion ceiling is a combined $916.9 billion reduction in Medicaid payments to providers from the two sections, as the rules would implement them, less $163.7 billion in lower provider taxes. [I]

The $488 billion floor subtracts at most a further $264.7 billion: the state share of the SDP cut, which providers may partly stop funding through provider taxes and intergovernmental transfers (IGTs). [I] THEIA Services found no current public source showing how much of each state’s SDP funding comes from each source, or how states will fund SDPs going forward, so CMS’s data cannot narrow the range. [I]

Hospitals can still gauge their own exposure: a hospital’s SDP receipts and its provider tax and transfer payments allow a hospital-specific estimate (sec. 7), and state decisions make the result a range, not a single number. [I]

Both rules remain proposals that CMS can change before finalizing (sec. 1) [E], and THEIA Services takes no position on whether either is good policy.

# 1. The OBBBA and the two proposed rules

The OBBBA, formally Public Law 119-21 and called the Working Families Tax Cut legislation by CMS (91 FR 46562), sets two limits on how states fund Medicaid; CMS has proposed one rule for each, and both rules add provisions the OBBBA does not require. [E] A third, Section 71117, is carried out by a final rule (CMS-2448-F) that CMS’s provider tax estimates take into account and that CMS says does not significantly change them (91 FR 46590 n. 23, 46592; Appendix C). [E]

## The SDP rule (CMS-2449-P)

SDPs are extra payments, most approved in advance by CMS, that a state requires its Medicaid managed care plans to make to particular providers (42 CFR 438.6(c)(1); 91 FR 30447). [E] Most go to hospitals: hospitals received 87.5 percent of the estimated $143.8 billion in SDPs that needed CMS’s written prior approval in the federal fiscal year ending September 30, 2025 (91 FR 30447). [E] That is a share of one year’s payments, not of the projected cuts. [I]

## The provider tax rule (CMS-2452-P)

States tax hospitals, managed care plans, nursing facilities and other providers to fund part of their Medicaid share, collecting a projected $98.6 billion in calendar year 2026 (91 FR 46589). [E] Hospitals pay the largest share, $61.8 billion (Table 4, 91 FR 46589; Appendix B). [E]

Federal law bars states from promising providers their tax back, an arrangement called a hold harmless (Appendix B). [E] Under current rules a tax that raises more than a threshold share of taxed providers’ net patient revenue, at most 6 percent, is also an indirect hold harmless if, in the aggregate, 75 percent of taxpayers get back 75 percent or more of their tax (the 75/75 test; 91 FR 46564). [E] A failed tax costs the state federal matching funds on the revenue that does not qualify (42 CFR 433.70(b)) [E], so a lower threshold limits how much states can raise. [I]

# 2. What links the two rules

States can fund their Medicaid share, which the federal government matches, from general revenue, provider taxes, or IGTs: funds that units of government within a state, such as public hospitals, transfer as the non-federal share (Appendix B). [E] Between 80 and 90 percent of SDPs relied entirely or partly on provider taxes or IGTs by the end of 2025, on CMS’s preprint data (91 FR 30452; Appendix C). [E] The Government Accountability Office’s state figures for SDPs approved as of August 26, 2022 combine provider taxes and IGTs in one amount, not dollars by source, and GAO says that amount is undercounted (GAO-24-106202, Table 4). [E]

The financing source decides which rule reaches an SDP: a tax-funded SDP is capped by the SDP rule, and the provider tax rule limits the taxes that fund it, while an SDP funded only by IGTs faces the cap alone. [I]

Because provider taxes fund many SDPs and both rules reach tax-funded SDPs, CMS’s estimates overlap. [I] Under CMS’s central estimate, the provider tax rule would cut provider payments by $384.0 billion alone but by only $142.1 billion more once the SDP rule’s cuts are counted, and CMS attributes the $241.9 billion difference ($154.9 billion federal, $87.0 billion state) to cuts already counted in the SDP rule (91 FR 46592). [E] Any total that adds the two estimates without removing the $241.9 billion overlap counts $241.9 billion twice. [I]

The $241.9 billion overlap rests on two central assumptions: that 65 percent of SDPs are tax-financed, within CMS’s 55 to 75 percent estimate (its high-savings and low-savings values) for the tax-financed share of SDP spending, and that states apply at most 80 percent of the reductions to SDPs (91 FR 46592). [E] CMS’s other estimates move these assumptions together, so its figures do not isolate the effect of any one (Appendix D). [I]

# 3. CMS’s estimates for each rule

CMS estimates each rule in a Regulatory Impact Analysis measured against a projection of Medicaid without the OBBBA, for the SDP rule’s SDP estimates (91 FR 30447) and for the provider tax rule (91 FR 46589–46591) [E], so both include the OBBBA’s effect. [I] The SDP rule’s fee-for-service estimates state no baseline (91 FR 30455). [I] The SDP rule prices Section 71116 (Tables 18–20, 91 FR 30451–30452) and its extension to other services (91 FR 30453) separately [E]; THEIA Services found no separate estimate of what the provider tax rule adds to Section 71115. [I]

CMS publishes a central estimate (its primary scenario) and low-savings and high-savings estimates for each rule; for the SDP rule they vary the assumed gaps between commercial, Medicaid and Medicare rates (91 FR 30453–30454; Appendix D). [E] For the provider tax rule, besides the two assumptions behind the overlap (sec. 2), the central estimate assumes states offset 30 percent of the cut with other revenue; the other estimates change all three (91 FR 46591–46594). [E] CMS states the offset immediately before its $384.0 billion total, splits the $142.1 billion and $241.9 billion from that total and prints no total before the offset (91 FR 46591–46592) [E]; THEIA Services reads all three figures as net of the offset, which CMS does not say in words. [I]

CMS states each scenario’s assumptions as single values, not by state (91 FR 30453–30454, 46593–46594). [E] THEIA Services reads them as national, so the estimates express uncertainty about national averages; it found no basis for assigning any state to one scenario. [I]

# 4. The net effect on providers

Right after its +$21.7 billion figure for the provider tax rule, CMS says that, taken together, the two proposed rules would on net still leave Medicaid paying providers significantly less over time (91 FR 46593). [E] The statement is qualitative and gives no combined figure. [I]

Combined, the two rules would leave providers about $488 billion to $753 billion worse off over 2026–2035 under CMS’s central estimates. [I] The gap between the two ends comes from step 3 alone: how much of the state share of SDPs providers fund themselves, which THEIA Services found in no public source. [I] Both ends rest on CMS’s central assumptions about how states respond (sec. 3; sec. 7, step 3). [I]

Step 3 rests on CMS’s caveat that, where providers pay the provider taxes or IGTs that fund the state share of SDPs, “the net change in provider revenue … could be significantly less,” though CMS is “unable to estimate the impacts on providers in more detail” (91 FR 30453). [E]

The $488 billion is a best case, since states fund part of the state share from general revenue and step 2’s $163.7 billion may already count part of what providers stop paying; CMS’s data support no closer estimate. [I] CMS’s low-savings and high-savings estimates, paired by THEIA Services, widen the range to about $122 billion (low-savings, after step 3) at one end and about $1,126 billion (high-savings, after step 2) at the other, in 2026 dollars over 2026–2035 (Appendix D). [I]

CMS’s figures cover all providers, with no separate hospital figure; for the SDP rule CMS says it cannot attribute the impacts by provider type (91 FR 30462), and for the provider tax rule it expects the largest decreases to fall on hospitals (91 FR 46591). [E] Hospitals are projected to pay $61.8 billion of the $98.6 billion in provider taxes in calendar year 2026 (Table 4, 91 FR 46589) [E]; if that one-year share held for the decade, hospitals would also receive most of step 2’s $163.7 billion in tax relief, an allocation that is THEIA Services’ assumption, not CMS’s. [I]

## KFF’s $155 billion

KFF’s $155 billion, from its August 14, 2026 analysis of SDPs in 37 states, is the part of the SDP rule’s $510 billion federal cut that KFF reports is also counted in the provider tax rule. [E] That matches CMS’s $154.9 billion federal share of the $241.9 billion overlap (91 FR 46592). [I] KFF and CMS therefore report one finding, federal only versus federal plus state. [I]

## HFMA’s $56.6 billion

HFMA’s $56.6 billion appears, on THEIA Services’ reading, to subtract the $163.7 billion in lower provider taxes twice. [I] HFMA’s July 22, 2026 article, which describes the same no-OBBBA comparison point as CMS, reported that provider payments would fall $220.3 billion over 10 years and providers would save $163.7 billion in taxes, “leaving a net decrease of $56.6 billion.” [E] But $220.3 billion matches the provider line of CMS’s Table 12, which already subtracts the $163.7 billion [I]; THEIA Services found $56.6 billion in neither rule [I], and if this reading is right, HFMA understates the provider tax rule’s standalone net effect, a $220.3 billion loss. [I]

CMS’s wording may explain the reading. [I] CMS describes the $384.0 billion payment cut as shown in Table 12 (91 FR 46592), although the $384.0 billion appears in Table 11 and Table 12 shows the net −$220.3 billion. [E] Of the five comments on the provider tax rule that cite $220.3 billion (five distinct texts, the same as filed and corrected), four describe it as a payment cut (comments CMS-2026-2476-0049, -0085, -0190 and -0201) and one as net of lower taxes (-0212); none arrives at $56.6 billion. [E] Georgetown University’s Center for Children and Families reported the same $220.3 billion on July 27, 2026 as already net of providers’ lower taxes [E], and HFMA had not replied to THEIA Services’ request for confirmation, sent [DATE SENT], as of [DATE]. [I]

## POLITICO’s $681 billion and the Congressional Budget Office’s estimates

POLITICO’s $681 billion compares two estimates of the same OBBBA provisions on mismatched bases. [I] Its September 2, 2026 article reported that hospitals had expected to lose $340 billion through 2034 under the 2025 law, that the rules would produce about $756 billion in federal and $265 billion in state savings through 2035, and that, “as hospitals see it,” hospitals are losing $681 billion more than expected. [E]

The article names no source for the $340 billion. [I] The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimated that Section 71115 would reduce federal Medicaid outlays by $191.1 billion and Section 71116 by $149.4 billion over fiscal years 2025–2034 (July 21, 2025, Table 7, Title VII sheet; for Section 71116 also October 28, 2025, p. 5). [E] CBO reports interactions among the OBBBA’s Medicaid provisions separately (October 28, 2025, p. 6). [E] POLITICO’s $340 billion matches the sum of the two section figures, $340.5 billion, which CBO does not print. [I]

POLITICO’s CMS inputs mix scopes. [I] Its $756 billion equals both rules’ federal savings measured alone ($510.1 billion + $245.8 billion, 2026–2035, 2026 dollars). [I] Like CBO’s section figures, on THEIA Services’ reading of CBO’s tables, it leaves out the interaction between the two sections, but it covers other years, uses another baseline and may differ in dollar basis. [I] Read as the two rules’ combined cut, it counts the $154.9 billion federal overlap twice. [I] Its $265 billion is the SDP rule’s state spending reduction alone (sec. 3). [I] A gap between government savings estimates measures provider payments before tax relief, across all provider types, not a net hospital loss. [I]

On THEIA Services’ reading of CBO’s table layout (CBO does not say so in words), CBO’s section figures exclude the interaction between the sections. [I] The CMS figure treated the same way is its federal total without the interaction, $755.9 billion for 2026–2035; CMS’s $601.0 billion has no CBO counterpart (Appendix F). [I] Of the known differences, only CMS’s extra year lowers its figure, and removing the interaction raises it. [I] Without 2035, the $601.0 billion becomes $501.5 billion and the no-interaction total $630.9 billion (federal, 2026–2034), nine years against CBO’s ten. [I] Year bases may also differ (sec. 3). [I] Three other differences have no known direction: CMS’s 2026 dollars against CBO’s unstated dollar basis, what the provider tax rule adds to Section 71115, and the agencies’ comparison projections. [I] So the rest of the gap between these published estimates is unresolved. [I] THEIA Services found no independent derivation of $681 billion before September 2, 2026 [I]; one later comment restates it (comment CMS-2026-2476-0079; Appendix G). [E]

## ASPE’s $891 billion

ASPE’s $891 billion, from an August 12, 2026 report by HHS’s Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE), is a combined federal and state Medicaid spending cut from the OBBBA’s provider tax and SDP provisions over 2025–2034, used to estimate benefits to other payers (p. 11) [E]; ASPE does not say whether it removes the overlap between the two provisions. [I]

ASPE estimates up to 3.5 percent lower non-Medicaid prices [E], the counterpart of the $35.0 billion in tax relief CMS assigns to private payers [I], and $502 billion to $875 billion in benefits to non-Medicaid payers over 2025–2034 (pp. 10–11). [E] ASPE scored the OBBBA’s provisions directly rather than as CMS’s proposed rules would implement them, over different years, so its $891 billion cannot be compared directly with THEIA Services’ combined $916.9 billion. [I]

# 7. Estimating one hospital’s position

A hospital can estimate its own position with the same three steps, because its own records hold the two figures public data lack: its SDP receipts and its provider tax and IGT payments. [I]

## Step 1. SDP revenue at risk

For each SDP program, the hospital adds its base managed care payment and its SDP for each service, compares the total with the cap (100 percent of Medicare in expansion states, 110 percent elsewhere), and treats the SDP amount above the cap as at risk. [I] Grandfathered programs fall by 10 percentage points of Medicare a year until they reach the cap, starting with the state’s first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028, while other programs get no phase-down (Section 71116; sec. 1). [E] The hospital’s own records and the state’s documents supply the inputs (Appendix C). [I] Step 1 covers only SDP amounts above the cap; it leaves out cuts that follow lost provider tax revenue, including the share of the provider tax rule’s cut that falls outside SDPs (at least 20 percent under CMS’s 80 percent ceiling; sec. 2), which a hospital should treat as a separate exposure. [I]

## Step 2. Lower provider taxes

Step 2 is the drop in the hospital’s own provider tax bill. [I] It is usually zero in non-expansion states, though a tax enacted or raised after July 4, 2025 may fall [I]: CMS says new taxes would in some cases be ineligible to remain in place beyond October 1, 2026 (91 FR 46589). [E] Step 2 is zero for a tax already at or below the threshold. [I] In expansion states, the hospital estimates its bill at each step of the lower threshold, 5.5 percent in federal fiscal year 2028 down to 3.5 percent from 2032, assuming the state lowers the tax to comply; the threshold limits a tax class’s revenue as a share of the class’s net patient revenue, not the hospital’s own rate, so the state’s plan for the tax decides the bill. [I] Until CMS provides each class’s new threshold, which it intends to do by September 30, 2028 (91 FR 46574) [E], the state’s current tax is the starting point. [I] Step 2 is not directly comparable with the national $163.7 billion over 2026–2035, which is measured against a projection without the OBBBA, comes from $198.7 billion in lower collections that include $51.0 billion of tax increases avoided under the freeze, and is what remains after the $35.0 billion CMS assigns to private payers (sec. 3). [I]

## Step 3. Less money paid to fund SDPs

Step 3 is the money the hospital might stop paying to fund the state share of SDPs. [I] That money flows through provider taxes or through IGTs a public hospital or its owning local government makes. [I] It depends on a decision the state has not made, so the hospital models three cases: [I]

-   The state lowers the tax or transfer as SDPs shrink, maximizing the hospital’s step 3 saving.

-   The state keeps collecting the tax for other Medicaid costs, leaving no step 3 saving.

-   The state replaces part of the lost SDPs with general revenue or other payment programs, shrinking the step 1 loss. CMS’s 30 percent offset covers only the provider tax rule’s cuts (sec. 3): replacing those cuts beyond 30 percent departs from CMS’s assumption, while replacing SDP payments lost to the cap is relief the national range does not include. [I]

Step 3 must not count a tax cut already counted in step 2. [I] A state’s preprint, the form it submits for CMS’s approval of an SDP, lists funding sources by type and reports IGT dollars by transferring entity but no provider tax dollars (SDP preprint, Section VI). [E] A state hospital association that works with the state on its provider tax program may hold member-level models of the tax and its payment pool. [I]

## Questions to ask the state Medicaid agency

-   Which SDP programs does the state treat as grandfathered, when does its first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028 begin, and how will it calculate each program’s Medicare-rate cap?

-   Which provider taxes and IGTs fund each program, and in what amounts?

-   Will the state lower provider tax rates as SDPs shrink, keep collecting the taxes for other Medicaid costs, or replace lost SDPs from other sources?

-   Will the state redesign or resubmit any program before the cap applies?

The three steps cover only the two rules, leaving out managed care contracting, commercial price changes and the OBBBA’s other Medicaid provisions, including eligibility changes that affect how many Medicaid patients a hospital treats. [I]

# 8. Services already under strain

Obstetric, pediatric and inpatient psychiatric services were shrinking or shifting before either rule was proposed (studies below) [E], and the rules’ effect on them cannot be predicted from public data because it depends on state budgets, managed care contracts and each hospital’s finances. [I] THEIA Services makes no prediction about closures or service cuts.

-   **Obstetrics.** GAO reported research showing that the number of rural hospitals providing obstetric services fell from 2004 through 2018, and that by 2018 more than half of rural counties had no hospital offering them (GAO-23-105515, October 2022). [E]

-   **Pediatrics.** A national study found that pediatric inpatient units fell 29.9 percent and pediatric beds 19.5 percent from 2008 to 2022, against a 4.4 percent decline in adult units (Michelson et al., December 2024). [E]

*[Figure 7] Pediatric inpatient units fell faster than adult units from 2008 to 2022, in one national study.*

-   **Inpatient psychiatry.** Psychiatric beds per 100,000 people fell from 11.3 to 9.06 in short-term acute care hospitals and rose from 16.8 to 19.5 in freestanding psychiatric hospitals between 2011 and 2023; the total stayed about flat at 28.1 and 28.4 (Lindenfeld et al., PLoS Medicine, July 2025). [E] The average annual percent change was not significant for any type of bed, although the authors’ regression models show a significant yearly decline in general hospitals’ psychiatric beds. [E]

The direction of the psychiatric estimates matters for adult Medicaid patients, because a freestanding psychiatric hospital with more than 16 beds meets the definition of an institution for mental diseases (IMD; Appendix B). [I] Federal Medicaid payment for IMD patients aged 21 to 64 is restricted by Social Security Act § 1905(a), with an exception for managed care (42 CFR 438.6(e)); the restriction long predates both rules. [E] Where hospitals run obstetric, pediatric or psychiatric services below cost and cover the loss from other revenue, lower SDPs are a plausible pressure point. [C]

*[Figure 8] Psychiatric beds per 100,000 people by hospital type, 2011–2023. The combined total was about flat, and the average annual percent change was not significant for any type of bed.*

## Rural hospitals have more time, not immunity

The OBBBA gives rural hospitals, as it defines them (Appendix B), until July 4, 2025, rather than May 1, for SDPs approved or sought through a documented good-faith effort, but their grandfathered payments still phase down from 2028 like other hospitals’ (Section 71116; sec. 1). [E]

For critical access, freestanding children’s and certain cancer hospitals, CMS proposes to set the SDP limit from each hospital’s most recent Medicare cost report; a default to the state plan rate was an alternative it did not propose (91 FR 30414). [E] Virginia’s Medicaid agency urged CMS not to default payment limits to state plan rates for services without a Medicare equivalent (comment CMS-2026-1916-0755, p. 3). [E] THEIA Services does not assess whether rural hospitals are less able to absorb these changes; the studies cited track services, not hospital finances. [I]

## B. Terms

**State directed payments (SDPs).** Extra payments a state requires its Medicaid managed care plans to make to particular providers (42 CFR 438.6(c)(1)). [E] A state asks CMS to approve an SDP on a form called a preprint, and a **rating period** is the period for which a state sets its plans’ payment rates. [I] A **grandfathered** SDP is one approved, or sought through a documented good-faith effort, before May 1, 2025 (by July 4, 2025 for rural hospitals); it may stay above the cap until it is phased down (sec. 1). [E]

**Expansion states.** States that cover low-income adults under Social Security Act § 1902(a)(10)(A)(i)(VIII). [E]

**Intergovernmental transfers (IGTs).** Funds transferred from or certified by units of government within a state as the non-federal share of Medicaid spending (Social Security Act § 1903(w)(6)(A)). [E]

**Provider taxes.** Taxes states levy on providers to fund part of their Medicaid share. CMS projects $98.6 billion in calendar year 2026, about 26 percent of the roughly $376 billion states will spend on Medicaid medical assistance: hospitals $61.8 billion, managed care organizations $28.1 billion, nursing and intermediate care facilities $7.3 billion, and all other providers $1.4 billion (Table 4, 91 FR 46589). [E] Of the $198.7 billion fall in collections over 2026–2035, $51.0 billion comes from the freeze and $147.7 billion from the lower threshold (Tables 6–8, 91 FR 46589–46590). [E] A **permissible class** is a category of items or services a state may tax, and CMS intends to give each state a threshold for each class (91 FR 46574). [E]

**Hold harmless and the 75/75 test.** Federal law bars a state from guaranteeing to hold taxpayers harmless for their tax (Social Security Act § 1903(w)(4)(C)(i); 42 CFR 433.68(f)(3)). [E] Under the 75/75 test, a tax above the threshold is also a hold harmless if, in the aggregate, 75 percent of taxpayers get back 75 percent or more of their tax (91 FR 46564). [E] A tax that fails costs the state federal matching funds, because CMS deducts the non-qualifying revenue from the state’s Medicaid spending before it calculates the federal match (42 CFR 433.70(b)). [E]

**Threshold and freeze.** The **threshold** is the most a tax may raise as a share of the taxed class’s net patient revenue; the OBBBA lowers it in expansion states from federal fiscal year 2028, and the **freeze** holds provider taxes in every state at their July 4, 2025 levels (sec. 1). [E] Taxes on nursing facilities and on intermediate care facilities for people with intellectual disabilities are exempt from the lower threshold (P.L. 119-21, § 71115(a)(2), 139 Stat. 302). [E]

**Central, low-savings and high-savings estimates.** CMS’s primary scenario and its two alternative scenarios for each rule (sec. 3; Appendix D). [E] The **overlap** is the part of the provider tax rule’s cut that CMS counts in the SDP rule’s estimate, $241.9 billion in the central estimate (91 FR 46592). [E] This note calls CMS’s adjustment for that overlap the **interaction**. [I]

**Federal fiscal year.** October 1 to September 30, named for the year in which it ends (31 U.S.C. 1102). [E]

**Institutions for mental diseases (IMDs).** Facilities of more than 16 beds primarily engaged in caring for people with mental diseases (Social Security Act § 1905(i)). [E] Federal Medicaid payment for IMD patients aged 21 to 64 is restricted by § 1905(a), which reaches patients under 65 but excepts inpatient psychiatric care for those under 21 (§ 1905(a)(16)). [E] The managed care exception for enrollees aged 21–64 is at 42 CFR 438.6(e), and section 1115 demonstrations for substance use disorder treatment provide a further exception (81 FR 27562). [E] THEIA Services found no source for the share of freestanding psychiatric hospitals that are IMDs, so sec. 8 relies on the definition alone. [I]

**Rural hospitals.** For the later grandfathering date, the OBBBA counts rural acute care hospitals paid under Medicare’s inpatient prospective payment system, and critical access, sole community, Medicare-dependent small rural, low-volume and rural emergency hospitals (P.L. 119-21, § 71116(d)(2), 139 Stat. 304). [E]

## C. Limits of the analysis and the five data gaps

Five gaps in public data stand between a state’s exposure and a hospital’s loss. [I]

1. **Which hospitals receive which SDPs.** THEIA Services located no nationally standardized public dataset that links SDPs to the facilities that receive them. [I] This is the facility-level limit: national and state figures cannot be divided among hospitals. [I]
2. **What each hospital pays in provider taxes and IGTs.** Neither Regulatory Impact Analysis, nor the CMS-64 and MACPAC materials THEIA Services reviewed, publishes provider tax payments by facility. [I] The net provider figure is a payment cut less a tax cut, and neither part can be observed hospital by hospital. [I]
3. **How states fund SDPs, in dollars.** THEIA Services located no current public source giving SDP financing in dollars by source and by state. [I] The Government Accountability Office’s state figures for SDPs approved as of August 26, 2022 give provider taxes and IGTs as one combined amount, because states do not report the amount from each source, and GAO says the amount is undercounted (GAO-24-106202, December 2023, app. III, Table 4, pp. 44–46). [E] For SDPs projected above 100 percent of Medicare, CMS reports that 39.8 percent were funded partly or wholly by IGTs but not provider taxes, 26.9 percent by provider taxes but not IGTs, and 14.2 percent by both, based on its June 2025 analysis of 402 SDPs it approved from 2022 through 2024 (91 FR 30410 and footnote 40). [E] CMS does not say whether these shares count SDPs or dollars. [I] The SDP preprint’s financing section asks states to check off funding sources and to report IGT dollars by transferring entity, but asks for no provider tax dollars (CMS, 42 CFR 438.6(c) preprint, January 2021, Section VI, questions 34–36). [E] This gap is why the headline is a range (sec. 4). [I]
4. **Each state’s binding threshold.** States would file interim provider tax data by December 31, 2026, from which CMS would calculate an interim threshold for planning (91 FR 46583), and CMS intends to provide each state’s threshold for each permissible class no later than September 30, 2028 (91 FR 46574). [E] Until then the threshold that binds a given state and class is not public. [I]
5. **CMS’s price ratios.** THEIA Services did not check CMS’s commercial-to-Medicare and Medicaid-to-Medicare ratios, which the SDP rule varies across scenarios that run from $408.4 billion to $989.7 billion (Appendix D). [I] Public commercial price sources exist, notably hospital price transparency files and RAND’s studies of employer-plan hospital prices, and were not searched. [I]

**Other limits.** The note gives no nominal-dollar or annual view of CMS’s figures and no estimate of effects on commercial prices or uncompensated care. [I] CMS’s figures are in 2026 dollars, and CMS assigns $35.0 billion of the lower provider taxes to private payers (Table 8, 91 FR 46590). [E] The provider tax rule’s full posted comment record was read for the figures and claims in sec. 5 (Appendix I). [I]

**Section 71117.** This section governs waivers of the requirement that provider taxes be uniform, and is carried out by the final rule CMS-2448-F (91 FR 4794). [E] CMS lists it as an interaction its provider tax estimates take into account (91 FR 46590 n. 23, 46592). [E]

**Inputs for a hospital’s estimate (sec. 7).** Managed care remittances show what the hospital receives. [I] The state’s approved preprint shows each program’s design and funding sources, and the state’s managed care contracts show when rating periods begin. [I]

## H. State group definitions and KFF data

**The groups.** THEIA Services sorts the 50 states and DC by expansion status (KFF’s expansion tracker), by whether KFF finds hospital SDPs above the new caps (KFF, August 14, 2026) and by whether KFF finds a non-exempt provider tax above 3.5 percent (KFF, August 20, 2026). [I]

- **A.** Expansion states with hospital SDPs above the caps and a hospital tax above 3.5 percent: 24 states, about $45.4 billion of one year’s federal hospital SDP spending above the caps. [I] Minnesota and Missouri belong here but have no KFF estimate. [I]
- **B.** Expansion states with hospital SDPs above the caps and a non-exempt tax above 3.5 percent on ambulances or another non-hospital class, but no hospital tax above it: Washington and West Virginia, about $2.0 billion. [I]
- **C.** Expansion states with hospital SDPs above the caps and no non-exempt tax above 3.5 percent: DC, Delaware and Hawaii, about $0.8 billion. [I]
- **D.** Non-expansion states with hospital SDPs above the caps: Florida, Georgia, Kansas, Mississippi, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas and Wisconsin, about $11.9 billion. [I]
- **E.** The 14 other states, with no hospital SDPs above the caps in KFF’s figures. [I] Five are expansion states the lower threshold reaches: Arkansas through an ambulance tax, and Colorado, Connecticut, Indiana and Vermont through hospital taxes. [I]

The groups cover 51 jurisdictions and about $60.1 billion, and KFF does not use them; rebuilt from KFF’s chart data, the group totals are $45.43 billion, $1.96 billion, $0.80 billion, $11.91 billion and $0. [I]

**What KFF’s data do and do not show.** KFF imputed missing payment rates in 15 states, three of them in Group D (Georgia, Texas and Wisconsin), and had complete payment rate data from the preprints for the rest (KFF, August 14, 2026, Methods). [E] KFF publishes no other state-level data-quality labels. [I] Florida, Kansas and Mississippi did not answer KFF’s 2025 provider tax survey (KFF, August 20, 2026). [E] Alabama and Wyoming have no Medicaid managed care on KFF’s map (KFF, August 14, 2026) [E], so they have no SDPs. [I] Colorado has an SDP above the new limits that KFF leaves out because CMS did not post its preprint until after July 20, 2026 (KFF, August 14, 2026, Figure 1 note) [E], so Group E’s $0 understates Colorado. [I]

**State figures.** The share of hospital SDP spending above the caps ranges from 9 percent in New Hampshire to 100 percent in Louisiana and DC, and is at least 70 percent in 27 states (KFF, August 14, 2026). [E] Texas has $3.5 billion above the caps, and Tennessee, Georgia and South Carolina more than $1.6 billion each (KFF, August 14, 2026). [E]
