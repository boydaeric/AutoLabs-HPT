**The Missing Total: What CMS’s Estimates Show for Medicaid Providers Under Two Proposed Rules [proposed title]**

*THEIA Services · Public note · September 6, 2026; revised [DATE]*

Evidence tags: [E] stated in a cited public document; [I] arithmetic or a reading by THEIA Services; [C] depends on facts not yet public.

# Executive summary

Under CMS’s central estimates, two proposed rules would leave providers about $488 billion to $753 billion worse off over 2026–2035, in 2026 dollars, net of the provider taxes they would no longer pay. [I] The rules would carry out two sections of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA): Section 71115 limits the taxes states levy on providers to fund Medicaid, and Section 71116 caps state directed payments (SDPs), extra payments a state requires its Medicaid managed care plans to make to particular providers. [E] CMS measures both rules against a projection of Medicaid without the OBBBA, so its estimates include the OBBBA’s own effect. [E] CMS publishes no hospital figure: it expects most of the SDP rule’s reductions to fall on hospitals, but says it lacks the data to attribute them by provider type. [E] A hospital’s own records can supply what CMS’s data cannot (sec. 7). [I]

THEIA Services found no published estimate of this combined net loss. [I] Published figures for the two rules run from $56.6 billion to $681 billion, and each measures something narrower or on a different basis, such as federal savings only or one rule alone. [I] CMS itself says the two rules together would still cut provider payments significantly over time [E], and THEIA Services found no CMS figure for that combined effect (sec. 4). [I]

The $753 billion ceiling is a combined $916.9 billion reduction in Medicaid payments to providers from the two sections, as the rules would carry them out, less $163.7 billion in lower provider taxes. [I]

The $488 billion floor subtracts at most a further $264.7 billion: the state share of the SDP cut, which providers may partly stop funding. [I] Between 80 and 90 percent of SDP arrangements rely entirely or partly on provider taxes or intergovernmental transfers (IGTs), money that local government bodies such as public hospitals transfer to the state. [E] Lower SDPs may therefore reduce the taxes and transfers that fund them. [I] THEIA Services found no current public source showing how much of each state’s SDP funding comes from each source, and states have not decided how they will fund SDPs going forward. [I] CMS’s data therefore cannot narrow the range. [I] The range also rests on CMS’s central assumption that states make up 30 percent of the provider tax rule’s cuts from other revenue [E], which THEIA Services reads as already inside CMS’s totals (sec. 4). [I]

Hospitals can still gauge their own exposure. [I] At the state level, exposure depends on Medicaid expansion status, how far the state’s SDPs exceed the new caps, and, in expansion states, whether provider taxes exceed 3.5 percent, the threshold reached in 2032. [I] State exposure shows where the rules reach, not how much any provider would lose. [I] A hospital’s own SDP receipts and its provider tax and transfer payments determine its position, and those records allow a hospital-specific estimate; state decisions make the result a range, not a single number. [I]

Both rules remain proposals that CMS can change before finalizing [E], and THEIA Services takes no position on whether either is good policy.

# 1. The OBBBA and the two proposed rules

The OBBBA, formally Public Law 119-21 and called the Working Families Tax Cut legislation by CMS (91 FR 46562), sets two limits on how states fund Medicaid; CMS has proposed one rule for each, and both rules add provisions the OBBBA does not require. [E] A third provision, Section 71117, sets requirements for waivers of the rule that provider taxes be uniform and is carried out by a rule CMS has already finalized (CMS-2448-F); CMS’s estimates for the provider tax rule take it into account, and CMS says it does not significantly change them (91 FR 46590 n. 23, 46592). [E] Comments closed July 21, 2026 for the SDP rule and September 21, 2026 for the provider tax rule, and both remain proposals. [E]

## The SDP rule (CMS-2449-P)

SDPs are extra payments, approved in advance by CMS, that a state requires its Medicaid managed care plans to make to particular providers (42 CFR 438.6(c)). [E] Most go to hospitals: hospitals received 87.5 percent of the estimated $143.8 billion in SDPs that needed CMS’s written prior approval in the federal fiscal year ending September 30, 2025 (91 FR 30447). [E] That is a share of one year’s payments, not of the projected cuts. [I] CMS published the SDP rule on May 22, 2026 (91 FR 30400). [E]

## The provider tax rule (CMS-2452-P)

States tax hospitals, managed care plans, nursing facilities and other providers to fund part of their Medicaid share, collecting a projected $98.6 billion in 2026, about 26 percent of the roughly $376 billion states will spend on Medicaid medical assistance (91 FR 46589). [E] Hospitals pay the largest share, $61.8 billion, followed by managed care organizations ($28.1 billion), nursing and intermediate care facilities ($7.3 billion) and all other providers ($1.4 billion) (Table 4, 91 FR 46589). [E]

Federal law and rules bar states from promising providers their tax back, an arrangement called a hold harmless (Social Security Act § 1903(w)(4)(C)(i); 42 CFR 433.68(f)(3)). [E] Under current rules, CMS also treats a tax as an indirect hold harmless when two conditions both hold: the tax raises more than a threshold share of taxed providers’ net patient revenue, at most 6 percent, and, in the aggregate, 75 percent of taxpayers get back 75 percent or more of their tax in Medicaid or other state payments (the 75/75 test; 91 FR 46564). [E] A failed tax costs the state federal matching funds on the revenue that does not qualify, because CMS deducts that revenue from the state’s Medicaid spending before calculating the federal match (42 CFR 433.70(b)) [E], so a lower threshold limits how much states can raise. [I] CMS published the provider tax rule on July 23, 2026 (91 FR 46562). [E]

# 2. What links the two rules

The two rules cut some of the same dollars because provider taxes fund many SDPs. [I]

States can fund their Medicaid share, which the federal government matches, from general revenue, provider taxes, or IGTs: funds that units of government within a state, such as public hospitals, transfer to the state as the non-federal share (Social Security Act § 1903(w)(6)(A)). [E] Between 80 and 90 percent of SDP arrangements relied entirely or partly on provider taxes or IGTs (91 FR 30452). [E] For SDPs projected above 100 percent of Medicare, CMS reports that 39.8 percent were funded partly or wholly by IGTs but not provider taxes, 26.9 percent by provider taxes but not IGTs, and 14.2 percent by both (91 FR 30410), shares that count arrangements, not dollars. [E] THEIA Services located no current public source of SDP financing in dollars by source and by state [I]; the most recent state-by-state dollar figures cover 2022 (Government Accountability Office, GAO-24-106202, Table 4). [E]

The financing source decides which rule reaches an SDP: a tax-funded SDP is capped by the SDP rule, and the provider tax rule limits the taxes that fund it, while an SDP funded only by IGTs faces the cap alone. [I]

Because both rules reach tax-funded SDPs, CMS’s estimates overlap. [I] Under CMS’s central estimate, which assumes states make up 30 percent of the cut from other revenue (sec. 3), the provider tax rule would cut provider payments by $384.0 billion alone but by only $142.1 billion more once the SDP rule’s cuts are counted, and CMS attributes the $241.9 billion difference ($154.9 billion federal, $87.0 billion state) to cuts already counted in the SDP rule (91 FR 46592). [E] Any total that adds the two estimates without removing the $241.9 billion overlap counts $241.9 billion twice. [I]

The $241.9 billion overlap rests on two central assumptions: that 65 percent of SDPs are tax-financed, within CMS’s 55 to 75 percent estimate for the tax-financed share of SDP spending, and that states apply at most 80 percent of the reductions to SDPs (91 FR 46592). [E] Other things equal, a higher tax-financed share means more overlap and a lower share less [I], but CMS’s other estimates move these assumptions together with the share of the cut states offset (91 FR 46593–46594) [E], so CMS’s figures do not isolate the effect of any one assumption. [I]

# 3. CMS’s estimates for each rule

CMS estimates each rule in a Regulatory Impact Analysis measured against a projection of Medicaid without the OBBBA, so both estimates include the OBBBA’s effect (91 FR 46589–46591) [E], and neither separates what the OBBBA requires from what CMS added. [I]

CMS publishes a central estimate (its primary scenario) and low-savings and high-savings estimates for each rule. [E]

-   **SDP rule.** The estimates vary assumed gaps between commercial, Medicaid and Medicare rates: for inpatient care, commercial insurers pay 1.89 times Medicare in the central estimate, 1.37 times in the low-savings estimate and 2.55 times in the high-savings estimate (Tables 14–16, 91 FR 30449–30450), which CMS describes as rates closer together (low-savings) and further apart (high-savings) (91 FR 30453–30454). [E]

-   **Provider tax rule.** The central estimate assumes that 65 percent of SDPs are tax-financed, that states apply at most 80 percent of the payment cut to SDPs, and that states offset 30 percent of the cut with other revenue (91 FR 46591–46592). [E] The low-savings estimate uses 75 percent, up to 90 percent and 40 percent; the high-savings estimate uses 55 percent, up to 70 percent and 20 percent; each also changes the share of provider taxes Medicaid pays (91 FR 46593–46594). [E] CMS states the 30 percent offset immediately before its $384.0 billion total, and splits the $142.1 billion and $241.9 billion from that total (91 FR 46591–46592). [E] CMS does not say in words that these figures are net of the offset; on that sequence, THEIA Services reads all three as already net of it. [I]

CMS applies each set of assumptions nationally [E], so the estimates express uncertainty about national averages, not differences among states, and THEIA Services found no basis for assigning any state to the low-savings or high-savings estimate. [I]

The federal and state reductions together equal the Medicaid payments each rule removes, so the totals are also payment cuts. [I] CMS describes the SDP rule’s reductions as falling on providers and other entities, such as local governments that reimburse providers for uncompensated care, and expects most of them to fall on hospitals; on the same page it says it lacks the data to attribute the impacts reliably to provider types, because SDPs are optional for states and their effects on providers are indirect (91 FR 30462). [E] The SDP rule’s low-savings and high-savings totals are $408.4 billion and $989.7 billion (91 FR 30453–30454). [E]

The provider tax rule also cuts state provider tax collections by $198.7 billion over 2026–2035, $51.0 billion from the freeze and $147.7 billion from the lower threshold (Tables 6–8, 91 FR 46589–46590), and CMS assigns $163.7 billion of the relief to providers and $35.0 billion to private payers, reasoning that providers pass part of their tax costs into commercial prices (Table 8, 91 FR 46590). [E]

For states, that lost tax revenue outweighs the lower spending. [I] CMS’s accounting statement for the provider tax rule shows states only as a net loss of $147.5 billion over 2026–2035, counting the SDP rule (Table 19, 91 FR 46596); measured alone, CMS puts the state net at a $60.5 billion loss (Table 12, 91 FR 46592). [E] Each net equals the state spending reduction in the table above, $51.2 billion or $138.2 billion, less the $198.7 billion in lost tax revenue. [I] The state column is therefore a spending reduction before lost tax revenue, not a state saving; the SDP rule’s $264.7 billion is likewise measured before any change in the provider taxes and IGTs that fund the state share. [I]

# 4. The net effect on providers

Right after its +$21.7 billion figure for the provider tax rule, CMS says that, taken together, the two proposed rules would on net still leave Medicaid paying providers significantly less over time (91 FR 46593). [E] The statement is qualitative and does not confirm any combined figure; THEIA Services found none from CMS. [I]

Combined, the two rules would leave providers about $488 billion to $753 billion worse off over 2026–2035 under CMS’s central estimates [I], a range driven entirely by how much of the state share of SDPs providers fund themselves, which THEIA Services found in no public source. [I] The range also rests on CMS’s central assumptions, including that states make up 30 percent of the provider tax rule’s cuts from other revenue (sec. 3), which THEIA Services reads as already inside the $142.1 billion. [I] A state that replaces more than CMS assumes would narrow the loss, and one that replaces less would widen it (sec. 7, step 3). [I]

| 3. Subtract the most providers could save by paying less to fund the state share of SDPs: the $264.7 billion state share of the SDP cut                          | 488.5                                           |

*Source: SDP rule, Tables 18 and 20, 91 FR 30451–30452; provider tax rule, 91 FR 46592 and Table 8, 91 FR 46590. Sums are THEIA Services’. The $264.7 billion is the sum of Table 20’s annual figures; CMS’s text gives $264.4 billion (Appendix E).*

Step 3 rests on CMS’s caveat that “to the extent providers pay provider taxes or IGTs that are used to support the non-Federal share for SDPs, the net change in provider revenue … could be significantly less,” though CMS is “unable to estimate the impacts on providers in more detail” (91 FR 30453). [E] Providers’ savings from paying less to fund the state share of SDPs cannot exceed $264.7 billion, the state share of the SDP cut. [I]

The $488 billion is a best case, since states fund part of the state share from general revenue and part of the money providers stop paying may already be counted in step 2’s $163.7 billion [I]; the actual loss lies above about $488 billion and at or below about $753 billion, and CMS’s data support no closer estimate. [I]

CMS’s lower-savings and higher-savings estimates widen the range to at least about $122 billion and at most about $1,126 billion. [I]

CMS does not say how the two rules’ estimates pair; pairing lower with lower and higher with higher is THEIA Services’ choice. [I]

CMS’s figures cover all providers, including hospitals, managed care organizations and nursing facilities, with no separate hospital figure. [E] CMS expects most of the SDP rule’s reductions to fall on hospitals but says, on the same page, that it cannot reliably attribute the impacts to provider types (91 FR 30462). [E] Hospitals are projected to pay $61.8 billion of the $98.6 billion in provider taxes in 2026 (Table 4, 91 FR 46589) [E]; if that one-year share held for the decade, hospitals would also receive most of step 2’s $163.7 billion in tax relief, an allocation that is THEIA Services’ assumption, not CMS’s. [I] No hospital figure follows from CMS’s data without assumptions like these; a hospital’s own records can supply one (sec. 7). [I]

## KFF’s $155 billion

KFF’s $155 billion, from its August 14, 2026 analysis of SDPs in 37 states, is the part of the SDP rule’s $510 billion federal cut that KFF reports is also counted in the provider tax rule. [E] That matches CMS’s $154.9 billion federal share of the $241.9 billion overlap (91 FR 46592). [I] KFF and CMS therefore report one finding, federal only versus federal plus state. [I]

## HFMA’s $56.6 billion

HFMA’s $56.6 billion appears, on THEIA Services’ reading, to subtract the $163.7 billion in lower provider taxes twice. [I] HFMA’s July 22, 2026 article, which describes the same no-OBBBA comparison point as CMS, reported that provider payments would fall $220.3 billion over 10 years and providers would save $163.7 billion in taxes, “leaving a net decrease of $56.6 billion.” [E] But $220.3 billion matches the provider line of CMS’s Table 12, which already subtracts the $163.7 billion [I]; THEIA Services found $56.6 billion in neither rule [E], and if this reading is right, HFMA understates the provider tax rule’s standalone net effect, a $220.3 billion loss. [I]

CMS’s wording may explain the reading. [I] CMS describes the $384.0 billion payment cut as shown in Table 12 (91 FR 46592), although the $384.0 billion appears in Table 11 and Table 12 shows the net −$220.3 billion. [E] HFMA’s reading is not unusual: of the five comments on the provider tax rule that cite $220.3 billion (five distinct texts, the same as filed and corrected), four, including a state Medicaid agency and a state hospital association, describe it as a payment cut, though none arrives at $56.6 billion. [E] Georgetown University’s Center for Children and Families reported the same $220.3 billion on July 27, 2026 as already net of providers’ lower taxes [E], and HFMA had not replied to THEIA Services’ request for confirmation, sent [DATE SENT], as of [DATE]. [E]

## POLITICO’s $681 billion and the Congressional Budget Office’s $340.5 billion

POLITICO’s $681 billion compares two estimates of the same OBBBA provisions on mismatched bases. [I] Its September 2, 2026 article reported that hospitals had expected to lose $340 billion through 2034 under the 2025 law, that the rules would produce about $756 billion in federal and $265 billion in state savings through 2035, and that, “as hospitals see it,” hospitals are losing $681 billion more than expected. [E]

The article names no source for the $340 billion. [E] The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimated that Section 71115 would reduce federal Medicaid outlays by $191.1 billion and Section 71116 by $149.4 billion over fiscal years 2025–2034, in dollars not adjusted for inflation (CBO, July 21, 2025, Table 7); CBO does not print their sum. [E] POLITICO’s $340 billion matches that sum, $340.5 billion, a federal-only figure set against CMS’s 2026–2035 estimates in 2026 dollars that include state spending. [I] CBO’s estimate also counts coverage effects, 1.1 million more people uninsured in 2034 from Section 71115 and none from Section 71116 (CBO, October 28, 2025, pp. 4–5), while CMS assumes the provider tax rule has no effect on enrollment (91 FR 46590). [E]

POLITICO’s CMS inputs also mix scopes: the $756 billion adds both rules’ federal savings ($510.1 billion + $245.8 billion) without removing the $154.9 billion federal overlap, and the $265 billion is the SDP rule’s reduction in state spending alone, omitting the provider tax rule’s $138.2 billion and the $198.7 billion in provider taxes states would no longer collect, which together leave states a net loss under that rule (sec. 3). [I] A gap between government savings estimates measures provider payments before tax relief, across all provider types, not a net hospital loss. [I]

Reconciling the published federal estimates on CMS’s own tables does not close the difference. [I] Counting federal spending only and removing the $154.9 billion federal overlap, CMS’s estimates total $601.0 billion for 2026–2035, against the $340.5 billion sum of CBO’s two estimates for 2025–2034. [I] Of the differences between them, only the extra year lowers CMS’s figure: dropping 2035 leaves $501.5 billion (Table 19, 91 FR 30452; Table 14, 91 FR 46593). [I] Without the interaction between the rules, CMS’s figure rises to $755.9 billion, or $630.9 billion without 2035 (Table 11, 91 FR 46591), and converting CMS’s 2026 dollars to CBO’s unadjusted dollars would raise it further by an amount CMS’s tables do not allow THEIA Services to compute. [I] The rest of the difference is unresolved on public data: CMS does not separate what the OBBBA requires from what it added, the two agencies use different comparison projections, and CBO counts coverage effects that CMS assumes away for the provider tax rule. [I] This is a reconciliation of published estimates; THEIA Services does not attribute the remaining difference to any one cause (Appendix F). [I] THEIA Services found no independent derivation of $681 billion before September 2, 2026; later references restate POLITICO’s figure (Appendix G). [E]

## ASPE’s $891 billion

ASPE’s $891 billion, from an August 12, 2026 report by HHS’s Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE), is a combined federal and state Medicaid spending cut from the OBBBA’s provider tax and SDP provisions over 2025–2034, used to estimate benefits to other payers (p. 11) [E]; ASPE does not say whether it removes the overlap between the two provisions. [E]

ASPE estimates up to 3.5 percent lower non-Medicaid prices [E], the counterpart of the $35.0 billion in tax relief CMS assigns to private payers [I], and $502 billion to $875 billion in benefits to non-Medicaid payers over 2025–2034 (pp. 10–11). [E] ASPE scored the OBBBA’s provisions directly rather than as CMS’s proposed rules would carry them out, over different years, so its $891 billion cannot be compared directly with THEIA Services’ combined $916.9 billion. [I]

Some organizations already net provider taxes against payments: CMS’s provider tax rule nets tax relief against payment cuts, and the Texas Hospital Association published paired gross and net figures in 2025. [E] A related request applies netting to a different calculation: the Federation of American Hospitals asked CMS to account for the provider taxes that partly fund SDPs when it calculates the total payment rate tested against the cap (comments on CMS-2449-P, July 21, 2026). [E] That is a test of payment rates, not a net provider loss (Appendix F). [I]

# 6. Where exposure is concentrated

THEIA Services groups states by which parts of the two rules reach them, showing where the rules apply, not how much any state would lose. [I] KFF’s August 14, 2026 analysis supplies the national picture: [E]

-   Hospital SDPs in 37 states, including DC, likely exceed the new caps. [E]

-   Excluding Minnesota and Missouri, for which KFF could not estimate an amount, about $60 billion of one year’s federal hospital SDP spending is above the caps, out of about $78 billion in federal hospital SDP spending and about $93 billion in all federal SDP spending. [E]

-   The share above the caps ranges from 9 percent in New Hampshire to 100 percent in Louisiana and DC, and is at least 70 percent in 27 states. [E]

-   31 expansion states have a non-exempt provider tax above 3.5 percent: 28 with hospital taxes, 10 with ambulance taxes and 6 with managed care organization taxes. [E]

KFF cautions that dollars above the caps do not show which states face the largest proportional effects, and finds that states with most SDP spending above the caps already have base rates at or near the caps, while low base rates leave more SDP spending under them. [E]

# 7. Estimating one hospital’s position

A hospital can estimate its own position with the same three steps, because its own records hold the two figures public data lack: its SDP receipts and its provider tax and IGT payments. [I] State decisions remain the main uncertainty, so the result is a range for each year, not a single number. [I]

## Step 1. SDP revenue at risk

For each SDP program, the hospital adds its base managed care payment and its SDP for each service, compares the total with the cap (100 percent of Medicare in expansion states, 110 percent elsewhere), and treats the SDP amount above the cap as at risk. [I] Grandfathered programs fall by 10 percentage points of Medicare a year until they reach the cap, starting with the state’s first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028, while other programs get no phase-down. [E] Managed care remittances show what the hospital receives; the state’s approved preprint, the form a state submits to get CMS’s written prior approval for an SDP, shows each program’s design; and the state’s managed care contracts show when rating periods begin. [I]

## Step 2. Lower provider taxes

Step 2 is the provider tax the hospital would no longer pay: its current tax bill minus its bill under each lower threshold. [I] The freeze holds the hospital’s tax rate at its July 4, 2025 level in every state [E], and in expansion states the hospital estimates its tax bill at each step of the lower threshold, from 5.5 percent of net patient revenue in federal fiscal year 2028 to 3.5 percent from 2032, assuming the state lowers its rate to comply. [I] CMS intends to announce final thresholds by state and tax class no later than September 30, 2028 (91 FR 46574) [E], so until then the state’s current tax structure for the hospital’s own tax class is the starting point. [I]

## Step 3. Less money paid to fund SDPs

Step 3 is the money the hospital might stop paying to fund the state share of SDPs. [I] That money flows through provider taxes or through IGTs a public hospital or its owning local government makes. [E] It depends on a decision the state has not made, so the hospital models three cases: [I]

-   The state lowers the tax or transfer as SDPs shrink, maximizing the hospital’s step 3 saving.

-   The state keeps collecting the tax for other Medicaid costs, leaving no step 3 saving.

-   The state replaces part of the lost SDPs with general revenue or other payment programs, shrinking the step 1 loss. CMS’s national estimate already assumes states make up 30 percent of the provider tax rule’s cuts from other revenue (sec. 4), so when comparing its result with the national range, a hospital should treat this case as a state departing from that assumption, not as relief on top of it. [I]

Step 3 must not count a tax cut already counted in step 2. [I] A state’s preprint lists funding sources by type and reports IGT dollars by transferring entity but no provider tax dollars [E], and a state hospital association that works with the state on its provider tax program may hold member-level models of the tax and its payment pool. [I]

## Questions to ask the state Medicaid agency

-   Which SDP programs does the state treat as grandfathered, and when does its first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028 begin?

-   How does the state calculate the Medicare rate it will use as each program’s cap?

-   Which provider taxes and IGTs fund each program, and in what amounts?

-   Will the state lower provider tax rates as SDPs shrink, keep collecting the taxes for other Medicaid costs, or replace lost SDPs from other sources?

-   Will the state redesign or resubmit any program before the cap applies?

The three steps cover only the two rules, leaving out managed care contracting, commercial price changes and the OBBBA’s other Medicaid provisions, including eligibility changes that affect how many Medicaid patients a hospital treats [I]; CMS assigns $35.0 billion of the lower provider taxes nationally to private payers, on the reasoning that providers pass part of their tax costs into commercial prices (Table 8, 91 FR 46590). [E]

# 8. Services already under strain

Obstetric, pediatric and inpatient psychiatric services were shrinking or shifting before either rule was proposed [E], and the rules’ effect on them cannot be predicted from public data because it depends on state budgets, managed care contracts and each hospital’s finances. [I] THEIA Services makes no prediction about closures or service cuts.

-   **Obstetrics.** GAO reported research showing that the number of rural hospitals providing obstetric services fell from 2004 through 2018, and that by 2018 more than half of rural counties had no hospital offering them (GAO-23-105515, October 2022). [E]

-   **Pediatrics.** A national study found that pediatric inpatient units fell 29.9 percent and pediatric beds 19.5 percent from 2008 to 2022, against a 4.4 percent decline in adult units (Michelson et al., December 2024). [E]

*[Figure 7] Pediatric inpatient units fell faster than adult units from 2008 to 2022, in one national study.*

-   **Inpatient psychiatry.** Psychiatric beds per 100,000 people fell from 11.3 to 9.06 in short-term acute care hospitals and rose from 16.8 to 19.5 in freestanding psychiatric hospitals between 2011 and 2023, while the total stayed about flat at 28.1 and 28.4 (Lindenfeld et al., PLoS Medicine, July 2025), with no statistically significant trend for any type of bed. [E]

The direction of the psychiatric estimates matters for adult Medicaid patients, because most freestanding psychiatric hospitals are institutions for mental diseases (IMDs), facilities of more than 16 beds primarily caring for people with mental diseases (Social Security Act § 1905(i)), where federal Medicaid payment for adults aged 21 to 64 is restricted (§ 1905(a)), with exceptions for managed care (42 CFR 438.6(e)) and certain demonstrations [I]; the restriction long predates both rules. [E] Where hospitals run obstetric, pediatric or psychiatric services below cost and cover the loss from other revenue, lower SDPs are a plausible pressure point. [C]

*[Figure 8] Psychiatric beds per 100,000 people by hospital type, 2011–2023. The combined total was about flat, and the authors found no statistically significant trend for any type of bed.*

## Rural hospitals have more time, not immunity

The OBBBA lets rural hospitals keep SDPs approved, or sought through a documented good-faith effort, by July 4, 2025, where other hospitals’ SDPs must date from before May 1, 2025, but their grandfathered payments still fall by 10 percentage points a year from each state’s first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028. [E] The OBBBA defines rural broadly: rural acute care hospitals paid under Medicare’s inpatient prospective payment system, and critical access, sole community, Medicare-dependent small rural, low-volume and rural emergency hospitals. [E]

Critical access, freestanding children’s and certain cancer hospitals are paid on cost and have no published Medicare rate, so their cap defaults to the state Medicaid plan rate [E], which may leave little or no room for SDPs above base rates. [I] State Medicaid agencies, including Virginia’s and Ohio’s, and many providers argued in comments on the SDP rule that this default would freeze payments at the levels SDPs were meant to correct (about 140 comments, about 100 distinct texts, the same as filed and corrected; keyword count). [E] THEIA Services does not assess whether rural hospitals are less able to absorb these changes; the studies cited track services, not hospital finances. [I]

# 9. What to watch

Each event below would change CMS’s estimates, change the rules, or show who bears the cuts. [I]

## Events that would change CMS’s estimates or the rules

1.  A final rule with a revised Regulatory Impact Analysis, especially any change to CMS’s 65 percent tax-financed assumption, which sets the size of the overlap between the rules, or to its assumed price ratios. [I]

2.  CMS’s analysis of how the two rules interact, which the SDP rule defers to future rulemaking [E] and the provider tax rule partly supplies. [I]

3.  Either rule’s arrival for review at the White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs, shown publicly on reginfo.gov [E], which signals that a final rule is near. [I]

4.  Publication of CMS-2450 (RIN 0938-AV70), a separate proposed rule under White House review since May 12, 2026, whose agenda entry states an intent to rescind or revise parts of CMS’s 2024 managed care rule, including SDP requirements. [E]

5.  CMS approvals or renewals of individual SDPs under current rules, which can bring financing effects forward before either rule is final: in September 2026, one state’s SDP programs lapsed at the start of its fiscal year during a dispute over its provider tax calculation, and CMS authorized them about two and a half weeks later (Texas Tribune, September 17, 2026; Fierce Healthcare, September 18, 2026; Appendix I). [E]

6.  A decision in Fifth Circuit case No. 25-40766 on whether CMS may treat redistribution arrangements among taxed providers as hold harmless arrangements, after the district court set aside the relevant 2024 rule provisions and 2023–2024 CMS guidance in September 2025. [E]

7.  Lawsuits or congressional action: as of September 22, 2026, THEIA Services located no lawsuit challenging either rule, and two July 2025 bills to repeal the underlying provisions, S. 2279 (Sections 71115 and 71116) and H.R. 4807 (Section 71115), had not advanced beyond committee referral (Appendix I). [E]

## Events that would show who bears the cuts

8.  Interim provider tax data states file with CMS for the quarter ending December 31, 2026, the first public basis for calculating a state’s lower threshold [I], ahead of final data due June 30, 2028 and CMS’s intended announcement of final thresholds by state and tax class no later than September 30, 2028 (91 FR 46574). [E]

9.  Any published analysis linking SDPs to the hospitals that receive them; no public source makes that link today. [E]

10. Any multi-state estimate that subtracts lower provider taxes from payment cuts for both rules over the same years; at least one state has published paired gross and net figures [E], but no multi-state version exists. [I]

11. State legislative and budget decisions on the lower threshold, which decide whether states absorb the cuts, replace the lost money or pass the cuts on to providers. [C]
