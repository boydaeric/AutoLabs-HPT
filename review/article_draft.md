**The Missing Total: What CMS’s Estimates Show for Medicaid Providers Under Two Proposed Rules [proposed title]**

*THEIA Services · Public note · September 6, 2026; revised [DATE]*

Evidence tags: [E] stated in a cited public document; [I] arithmetic or a reading by THEIA Services; [C] depends on facts not yet public.

# Executive summary

Providers may lose between $488 billion and $753 billion in Medicaid revenue over the next ten years, under CMS’s central estimates for two proposed rules carrying out One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) provisions that limit provider taxes and cap state directed payments (SDPs). [I] CMS publishes no hospital-specific figure but expects the majority of SDP cuts to fall on hospitals. [E]

THEIA Services found no published estimate of this combined net loss: figures in circulation range from $56.6 billion to $681 billion, a twelvefold spread, because each measures something narrower, such as federal savings, state savings or one rule’s effect alone. [I] Hospitals, states and policymakers are weighing the rules with figures that answer different questions, while the net loss to providers from both rules together goes unreported. [I]

The $753 billion ceiling reflects $163.7 billion in provider tax relief applied against a combined $916.9 billion provider payment reduction. [I]

Providers can then offset no more than $264.7 billion of the $753 billion loss: the state share of the eliminated SDPs, which providers may partly stop funding. [I] The $264.7 billion cap sets the $488 billion floor. [I] States fund SDPs from several sources: between 80 and 90 percent of SDP arrangements rely entirely or partly on provider taxes or intergovernmental transfers (IGTs), which can include transfers from public hospitals, and others use general revenue. [E] Lower SDPs may therefore reduce the provider taxes and transfers that fund them. [I] Public data do not show how much of each state’s SDP funding comes from each source, and states have not decided how they will fund SDPs going forward. [I] As a result, public data cannot narrow the $488 billion to $753 billion range. [I]

Providers can still gauge their own exposure. At the state level, exposure depends on Medicaid expansion status, how far the state’s SDPs exceed the new caps, and, in expansion states, whether provider taxes exceed the new 3.5 percent limit. [I] State exposure shows where the rules reach, not how much any provider would lose. [I] A provider’s own SDP receipts and its provider tax and transfer payments determine its position, and those records allow a provider-specific estimate. [I]

Both rules remain proposals that CMS can change before finalizing [E], and THEIA Services takes no position on whether either is good policy.

# 1. The OBBBA and the two proposed rules

The OBBBA, formally Public Law 119-21 and called the Working Families Tax Cut legislation by CMS, sets two limits on how states fund Medicaid; CMS has proposed one rule for each, and both rules add provisions the OBBBA does not require. [E] Comments closed July 21, 2026 for the SDP rule and September 21, 2026 for the provider tax rule, and both remain proposals. [E]

## The SDP rule (CMS-2449-P)

SDPs are extra payments, approved in advance by CMS, that a state requires its Medicaid managed care plans to make to particular providers, mostly hospitals; CMS published the SDP rule on May 22, 2026 (91 FR 30400). [E]

|------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| Caps SDPs for inpatient hospital, outpatient hospital, nursing facility and academic medical center practitioner services at 100 percent of Medicare rates in states that expanded Medicaid to low-income adults (expansion states) and 110 percent elsewhere.                                                      | Extends the cap to SDPs for other services: −$5.34 billion over 2029–2035 (91 FR 30453).                                     |

## The provider tax rule (CMS-2452-P)

States tax hospitals, managed care plans, nursing facilities and other providers to fund part of their Medicaid share, collecting a projected $98.6 billion in 2026, about 26 percent of the roughly $376 billion states will spend on Medicaid medical assistance (91 FR 46589). [E] Hospitals pay the largest share, $61.8 billion, followed by managed care organizations ($28.1 billion), nursing and intermediate care facilities ($7.3 billion) and all other providers ($1.4 billion) (Table 4, 91 FR 46589). [E]

Federal rules bar states from promising providers their tax back (a hold harmless), and CMS tests for an indirect promise in two parts: a tax fails if it raises more than a threshold share of taxed providers’ net patient revenue, 6 percent today, and most taxed providers also get most of their tax back through Medicaid. [E] A failed tax costs the state federal matching funds on the revenue that does not qualify [E], so a lower threshold limits how much states can raise. [I] CMS published the provider tax rule on July 23, 2026 (91 FR 46562). [E]

# 2. What links the two rules

The two rules cut some of the same dollars because provider taxes and IGTs fund many SDPs. [I]

States can fund their Medicaid share, which the federal government matches, from general revenue, provider taxes, or IGTs from local government bodies such as public hospitals, and 80 to 90 percent of SDPs relied entirely or partly on provider taxes or IGTs (91 FR 30452). [E] For SDPs projected above 100 percent of Medicare, CMS reports that 39.8 percent were funded partly or wholly by IGTs but not provider taxes, 26.9 percent by provider taxes but not IGTs, and 14.2 percent by both (91 FR 30410), shares that count arrangements, not dollars. [E] THEIA Services located no current public source of SDP financing in dollars by source and by state [I]; the most recent state-by-state dollar figures cover 2022 (GAO-24-106202, Table 4). [E]

The financing source decides which rule reaches an SDP: a tax-funded SDP is capped by the SDP rule and loses funding under the provider tax rule, while an SDP funded only by IGTs faces the cap alone. [I]

Because both rules reach tax-funded SDPs, CMS’s estimates overlap: the provider tax rule would cut provider payments by $384.0 billion alone but by only $142.1 billion more once the SDP rule’s cuts are counted, and CMS attributes the $241.9 billion difference ($154.9 billion federal, $87.0 billion state) to cuts already counted in the SDP rule (91 FR 46592). [E] Any total that adds the two estimates without removing the $241.9 billion overlap counts $241.9 billion twice. [I]

The $241.9 billion overlap rests on CMS’s assumption that 65 percent of SDPs are tax-financed, within CMS’s 55 to 75 percent estimate for the tax-financed share of SDP spending (91 FR 46592) [E]; a higher assumed share means more overlap, and a lower share less. [I]

# 3. CMS’s estimates for each rule

CMS estimates each rule in a Regulatory Impact Analysis measured against a projection of Medicaid without the OBBBA, so both estimates include the OBBBA’s effect (91 FR 46589–46591) [E], and neither separates what the OBBBA requires from what CMS added. [I]

CMS publishes a central estimate (its primary scenario) and low-savings and high-savings estimates for each rule. [E]

-   **SDP rule.** The estimates vary assumed gaps between commercial, Medicaid and Medicare rates: for inpatient care, commercial insurers pay 1.89 times Medicare in the central estimate, 1.37 times in the low-savings estimate and 2.55 times in the high-savings estimate (Tables 14–16, 91 FR 30449–30450), which CMS describes as rates closer together and further apart (91 FR 30453–30454). [E]

-   **Provider tax rule.** The estimates vary the tax-financed share of SDPs (75, 65 and 55 percent in the low-savings, central and high-savings estimates), the share of the payment cut falling on SDPs (up to 90 percent low-savings, up to 70 percent high-savings) and the share of the cut states offset (40 percent low-savings, 20 percent high-savings) (91 FR 46593–46594). [E]

CMS applies each set of assumptions nationally [E], so the estimates express uncertainty about national averages, not differences among states, and THEIA Services found no basis for assigning any state to the low-savings or high-savings estimate. [I]

Each rule’s federal and state savings equal the Medicaid payments it removes, so the totals are also payment cuts [I]; CMS describes the SDP rule’s savings as reductions to providers and other entities, such as local governments that reimburse providers for uncompensated care, with the majority falling on hospitals (91 FR 30462). [E] The SDP rule’s low-savings and high-savings totals are $408.4 billion and $989.7 billion (91 FR 30453–30454). [E]

The provider tax rule also cuts state provider tax collections by $198.7 billion over 2026–2035, $51.0 billion from the freeze and $147.7 billion from the lower threshold (Tables 6–8, 91 FR 46589–46590), and CMS assigns $163.7 billion of the relief to providers and $35.0 billion to private payers, reasoning that providers pass part of their tax costs into commercial prices (Table 8, 91 FR 46590). [E]

## POLITICO’s $681 billion and the Congressional Budget Office’s $340.5 billion

POLITICO’s $681 billion compares two estimates of the same OBBBA provisions on mismatched bases. [I] Its September 2, 2026 article reported that hospitals had expected to lose $340 billion through 2034, that the rules would produce about $756 billion in federal and $265 billion in state savings through 2035, and that, “as hospitals see it,” hospitals are losing $681 billion more than expected. [E]

The article names no source for the $340 billion [E], which matches the Congressional Budget Office’s $340.5 billion estimate of federal savings from Sections 71115 and 71116 ($191.1 billion and $149.4 billion; Congressional Research Service report R48633), a federal-only figure for 2025–2034 in dollars not adjusted for inflation [E], set against CMS’s 2026–2035 estimates in 2026 dollars that include state spending. [E] The Congressional Budget Office also scored the OBBBA itself, including enrollment effects CMS’s estimates do not model. [I]

POLITICO’s CMS inputs also mix scopes: the $756 billion adds both rules’ federal savings ($510.1 billion + $245.8 billion) without removing the $154.9 billion federal overlap, and the $265 billion is the SDP rule’s state savings alone, omitting the provider tax rule’s $138.2 billion. # 7. Estimating one hospital’s position

A hospital can estimate its own position with the same three steps, because its own records hold the two figures public data lack: its SDP receipts and its provider tax and IGT payments. [I] State decisions remain the main uncertainty, so the result is a range for each year, not a single number. [I]

## Step 1. SDP revenue at risk

For each SDP program, the hospital adds its base managed care payment and its SDP for each service, compares the total with the cap (100 percent of Medicare in expansion states, 110 percent elsewhere), and treats the SDP amount above the cap as at risk. [I] Grandfathered programs fall by 10 percentage points of Medicare a year until they reach the cap, starting with the state’s first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028, while other programs get no phase-down. [E] Managed care remittances show what the hospital receives, the state’s approved preprint shows each program’s design, and the state’s managed care contracts show when rating periods begin. [I]

## Step 2. Lower provider taxes

The freeze holds the hospital’s tax rate at its July 4, 2025 level in every state [E], and in expansion states the hospital estimates its tax bill at each step of the lower threshold, from 5.5 percent of net patient revenue in federal fiscal year 2028 to 3.5 percent from 2032, assuming the state lowers its rate to comply. [I] CMS announces final state thresholds on September 30, 2028 [E], so until then the state’s current tax structure is the starting point. [I]

## Step 3. Less money paid to fund SDPs

Step 3 is the money the hospital might stop paying to fund the state share of SDPs, through provider taxes or through IGTs a public hospital or its owning local government makes [E], and it depends on a decision the state has not made, so the hospital models three cases: [I]

-   The state lowers the tax or transfer as SDPs shrink, maximizing the hospital’s step 3 saving.

-   The state keeps collecting the tax for other Medicaid costs, leaving no step 3 saving.

-   The state replaces part of the lost SDPs with general revenue or other payment programs, shrinking the step 1 loss.

Step 3 must not count a tax cut already counted in step 2. [I] A state’s preprint lists funding sources by type and reports IGT dollars by transferring entity but no provider tax dollars [E], and a state hospital association that works with the state on its provider tax program may hold member-level models of the tax and its payment pool. [I]

## Questions to ask the state Medicaid agency

-   Which SDP programs does the state treat as grandfathered, and when does its first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028 begin?

-   How does the state calculate the Medicare rate it will use as each program’s cap?

-   Which provider taxes and IGTs fund each program, and in what amounts?

-   Will the state lower provider tax rates as SDPs shrink, keep collecting the taxes for other Medicaid costs, or replace lost SDPs from other sources?

-   Will the state redesign or resubmit any program before the cap applies?

The three steps cover only the two rules, leaving out managed care contracting, commercial price changes and the OBBBA’s other Medicaid provisions, including eligibility changes that affect how many Medicaid patients a hospital treats [I]; CMS assigns $35.0 billion of the lower provider taxes nationally to private payers, on the reasoning that providers pass part of their tax costs into commercial prices (Table 8, 91 FR 46590). [E]

# 8. Services already under strain

Obstetric, pediatric and inpatient psychiatric services were shrinking or shifting before either rule was proposed [E], and the rules’ effect on them cannot be predicted from public data because it depends on state budgets, managed care contracts and each hospital’s finances. [I] THEIA Services makes no prediction about closures or service cuts.

-   **Obstetrics.** The number of rural hospitals providing obstetric services fell from 2004 through 2018, and by 2018 more than half of rural counties had no hospital offering them (GAO-23-105515, October 2022). [E]

-   **Pediatrics.** Pediatric inpatient units fell 29.9 percent and pediatric beds 19.5 percent from 2008 to 2022, against a 4.4 percent decline in adult units (Michelson et al., December 2024). [E]

*[Figure 7] Pediatric inpatient units fell faster than adult units from 2008 to 2022, in one national study.*

-   **Inpatient psychiatry.** Psychiatric beds per 100,000 people fell from 11.3 to 9.06 in short-term acute care hospitals and rose from 16.8 to 19.5 in freestanding psychiatric hospitals between 2011 and 2023, leaving the total about flat at 28.1 and 28.4 (Lindenfeld et al., PLoS Medicine, July 2025), with no statistically significant trend for any type of bed. [E]

The direction of the psychiatric estimates matters for adult Medicaid patients, because most freestanding psychiatric hospitals are institutions for mental diseases, where federal Medicaid payment for adults aged 21 to 64 is restricted, with exceptions for managed care and certain demonstrations [I]; the restriction long predates both rules. [E] Where hospitals run obstetric, pediatric or psychiatric services below cost and cover the loss from other revenue, lower SDPs are a plausible pressure point. [C]

*[Figure 8] Psychiatric beds per 100,000 people by hospital type, 2011–2023. The combined total was about flat, and the authors found no statistically significant trend for any type of bed.*

## Rural hospitals have more time, not immunity

The OBBBA lets rural hospitals keep SDPs approved, or sought through a documented good-faith effort, by July 4, 2025, against May 1, 2025 for other hospitals, but their grandfathered payments still fall by 10 percentage points a year from each state’s first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028. [E] The OBBBA defines rural broadly: rural acute care hospitals paid under Medicare’s inpatient prospective payment system, and critical access, sole community, Medicare-dependent small rural, low-volume and rural emergency hospitals. [E]

Critical access, freestanding children’s and certain cancer hospitals are paid on cost and have no published Medicare rate, so their cap defaults to the state Medicaid plan rate [E], which may leave little or no room for SDPs above base rates. [I] THEIA Services does not assess whether rural hospitals are less able to absorb these changes; the studies cited track services, not hospital finances. [I]

# 9. What to watch

Each event below would change CMS’s estimates, change the rules, or show who bears the cuts. [I]

## Events that would change CMS’s estimates or the rules

1.  A final rule with a revised Regulatory Impact Analysis, especially any change to CMS’s 65 percent tax-financed assumption, which sets the size of the overlap between the rules, or to its assumed price ratios. [I]

2.  CMS’s analysis of how the two rules interact, which the SDP rule defers to future rulemaking [E] and the provider tax rule partly supplies. [I]

3.  Either rule’s arrival for review at the White House Office of Information and Regulatory Affairs, shown publicly on reginfo.gov [E], which signals that a final rule is near. [I]

4.  Publication of CMS-2450 (RIN 0938-AV70), a separate proposed rule under White House review since May 12, 2026, whose agenda entry states an intent to rescind or revise parts of CMS’s 2024 managed care rule, including SDP requirements. [E]

5.  CMS approvals or renewals of individual SDPs under current rules, which can bring financing effects forward: in September 2026, one state’s SDP programs lapsed at the start of its fiscal year during a dispute over its provider tax calculation, and CMS authorized them about two and a half weeks later (Texas Tribune, September 17, 2026; Fierce Healthcare, September 18, 2026). [E]

6.  A decision in Fifth Circuit case No. 25-40766 on whether CMS may treat redistribution arrangements among taxed providers as hold harmless arrangements, after the district court set aside the relevant 2024 rule provisions and 2023–2024 CMS guidance in September 2025. [E]

7.  Lawsuits or congressional action: as of September 22, 2026, THEIA Services located no lawsuit challenging either rule, and two July 2025 bills to repeal the underlying provisions, S. 2279 (Sections 71115 and 71116) and H.R. 4807 (Section 71115), had not advanced beyond committee referral. [E]

## Events that would show who bears the cuts

8.  Interim provider tax data states file with CMS for the quarter ending December 31, 2026, the first public basis for calculating a state’s lower threshold [I], ahead of final data due June 30, 2028 and CMS’s announcement of final thresholds by state and tax class on September 30, 2028. [E]

9.  Any published analysis linking SDPs to the hospitals that receive them; no public source makes that link today. [E]

10. Any multi-state estimate that subtracts lower provider taxes from payment cuts for both rules over the same years; at least one state has published paired gross and net figures [E], but no multi-state version exists. [I]

11. State legislative and budget decisions on the lower threshold, which decide whether states absorb the cuts, replace the lost money or pass the cuts on to providers. [C]
