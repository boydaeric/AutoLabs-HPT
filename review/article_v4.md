*THEIA Services · Public note · September 6, 2026; revised [DATE]*

# Executive summary

Under CMS’s central estimates, two proposed rules would leave providers about $488 billion to $753 billion worse off over 2026–2035, in 2026 dollars, net of lower provider taxes and, at the lower end, of the most providers could stop paying to fund the cut payments. [I] The rules would carry out two sections of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA): Section 71115 limits the taxes states levy on providers to fund Medicaid, and Section 71116 caps state directed payments (SDPs), extra payments a state requires its Medicaid managed care plans to make to particular providers (sec. 1). [E] CMS measures its SDP and provider tax estimates against a projection of Medicaid without the OBBBA (sec. 3) [E], so they include the OBBBA’s own effect. [I] CMS publishes no hospital figure: it expects most of the SDP rule’s reductions to fall on hospitals but says it lacks the data to attribute them by provider type (sec. 3). [E]

THEIA Services found no published estimate of this combined net loss. [I] The outside estimates this note examines, from the Healthcare Financial Management Association (HFMA), KFF and POLITICO, each measure something narrower or on a different basis, and CMS’s own figures and an $891 billion HHS estimate measure different things again (sec. 5). [I] CMS itself says the two rules together would on net still cut provider payments significantly over time (sec. 4) [E], without giving a combined figure. [I]

The $753 billion ceiling is a combined $916.9 billion reduction in Medicaid payments to providers from the two sections, as the rules would implement them, less $163.7 billion in lower provider taxes. [I]

The $488 billion floor subtracts at most a further $264.7 billion: the state share of the SDP cut, which providers may partly stop funding through provider taxes and intergovernmental transfers (IGTs). [I] THEIA Services found no current public source showing how much of each state’s SDP funding comes from each source, or how states will fund SDPs going forward, so CMS’s data cannot narrow the range. [I]

Hospitals can still gauge their own exposure: a hospital’s SDP receipts and its provider tax and transfer payments allow a hospital-specific estimate (sec. 7), and state decisions make the result a range, not a single number. [I]

Both rules remain proposals that CMS can change before finalizing (sec. 1) [E], and THEIA Services takes no position on whether either is good policy.

# 1. The OBBBA and the two proposed rules

The OBBBA, formally Public Law 119-21 and called the Working Families Tax Cut legislation by CMS (91 FR 46562), sets two limits on how states fund Medicaid; CMS has proposed one rule for each, and both rules add provisions the OBBBA does not require. [E] A third, Section 71117, is carried out by a final rule (CMS-2448-F) that CMS’s provider tax estimates take into account and that CMS says does not significantly change them (91 FR 46590 n. 23, 46592; Appendix C). [E]

## The SDP rule (CMS-2449-P)

SDPs are extra payments, most approved in advance by CMS, that a state requires its Medicaid managed care plans to make to particular providers (42 CFR 438.6(c)(1); 91 FR 30447). [E] Most go to hospitals: hospitals received 87.5 percent of the estimated $143.8 billion in SDPs that needed CMS’s written prior approval in the federal fiscal year ending September 30, 2025 (91 FR 30447). [E] That is a share of one year’s payments, not of the projected cuts. [I]

|------------------------------------|------------------------------------|
| Caps SDPs for inpatient hospital, outpatient hospital, nursing facility and academic medical center practitioner services at 100 percent of Medicare rates in states that expanded Medicaid to low-income adults (expansion states; Appendix B) and 110 percent elsewhere. | Extends the cap to SDPs for other services: CMS estimates −$5.34 billion in spending over 2029–2035 (91 FR 30453). |

## The provider tax rule (CMS-2452-P)

States tax hospitals, managed care plans, nursing facilities and other providers to fund part of their Medicaid share, collecting a projected $98.6 billion in calendar year 2026 (91 FR 46589). [E] Hospitals pay the largest share, $61.8 billion (Table 4, 91 FR 46589; Appendix B). [E]

Federal law bars states from promising providers their tax back, an arrangement called a hold harmless (Appendix B). [E] Under current rules a tax that raises more than a threshold share of taxed providers’ net patient revenue, at most 6 percent, is also an indirect hold harmless if, in the aggregate, 75 percent of taxpayers get back 75 percent or more of their tax (the 75/75 test; 91 FR 46564). [E] A failed tax costs the state federal matching funds on the revenue that does not qualify (42 CFR 433.70(b)) [E], so a lower threshold limits how much states can raise. [I]

# 2. What links the two rules

States can fund their Medicaid share, which the federal government matches, from general revenue, provider taxes, or IGTs: funds that units of government within a state, such as public hospitals, transfer as the non-federal share (Appendix B). [E] Between 80 and 90 percent of SDPs relied entirely or partly on provider taxes or IGTs by the end of 2025, on CMS’s preprint data (91 FR 30452; Appendix C). [E] THEIA Services located no current public source of SDP financing in dollars by source and by state [I]; the most recent state-by-state dollar figures cover 2022 (Government Accountability Office, GAO-24-106202, Table 4). [E]

The financing source decides which rule reaches an SDP: a tax-funded SDP is capped by the SDP rule, and the provider tax rule limits the taxes that fund it, while an SDP funded only by IGTs faces the cap alone. [I]

Because provider taxes fund many SDPs and both rules reach tax-funded SDPs, CMS’s estimates overlap. [I] Under CMS’s central estimate, the provider tax rule would cut provider payments by $384.0 billion alone but by only $142.1 billion more once the SDP rule’s cuts are counted, and CMS attributes the $241.9 billion difference ($154.9 billion federal, $87.0 billion state) to cuts already counted in the SDP rule (91 FR 46592). [E] Any total that adds the two estimates without removing the $241.9 billion overlap counts $241.9 billion twice. [I]

The $241.9 billion overlap rests on two central assumptions: that 65 percent of SDPs are tax-financed, within CMS’s 55 to 75 percent estimate (its high-savings and low-savings values) for the tax-financed share of SDP spending, and that states apply at most 80 percent of the reductions to SDPs (91 FR 46592). [E] Other things equal, a higher tax-financed share means more overlap and a lower share less [I], but CMS’s other estimates move these assumptions together with the share of the cut states offset (91 FR 46593–46594) [E], so CMS’s figures do not isolate the effect of any one assumption. [I]

# 3. CMS’s estimates for each rule

CMS estimates each rule in a Regulatory Impact Analysis measured against a projection of Medicaid without the OBBBA, for the SDP rule’s SDP estimates (91 FR 30447) and for the provider tax rule (91 FR 46589–46591) [E], so both include the OBBBA’s effect. [I] The SDP rule’s fee-for-service estimates state no baseline (91 FR 30455). [I] The SDP rule prices Section 71116 (Tables 18–20, 91 FR 30451–30452) and its extension to other services (91 FR 30453) separately [E]; THEIA Services found no separate estimate of what the provider tax rule adds to Section 71115. [I]

CMS publishes a central estimate (its primary scenario) and low-savings and high-savings estimates for each rule; for the SDP rule they vary the assumed gaps between commercial, Medicaid and Medicare rates (91 FR 30453–30454; Appendix D). [E] For the provider tax rule, besides the two assumptions behind the overlap (sec. 2), the central estimate assumes states offset 30 percent of the cut with other revenue; the other estimates change all three (91 FR 46591–46594). [E] CMS states the offset immediately before its $384.0 billion total, splits the $142.1 billion and $241.9 billion from that total and prints no total before the offset (91 FR 46591–46592) [E]; THEIA Services reads all three figures as net of the offset, which CMS does not say in words. [I]

CMS applies each set of assumptions nationally (91 FR 30453–30454, 46591–46594) [E], so the estimates express uncertainty about national averages, and THEIA Services found no basis for assigning any state to one scenario. [I]

# 4. The net effect on providers

Right after its +$21.7 billion figure for the provider tax rule, CMS says that, taken together, the two proposed rules would on net still leave Medicaid paying providers significantly less over time (91 FR 46593). [E] The statement is qualitative and gives no combined figure. [I]

Combined, the two rules would leave providers about $488 billion to $753 billion worse off over 2026–2035 under CMS’s central estimates. [I] The gap between the two ends comes from step 3 alone: how much of the state share of SDPs providers fund themselves, which THEIA Services found in no public source. [I] Both ends rest on CMS’s central assumptions about how states respond (sec. 3; sec. 7, step 3). [I]

Step 3 rests on CMS’s caveat that, where providers pay the provider taxes or IGTs that fund the state share of SDPs, “the net change in provider revenue … could be significantly less,” though CMS is “unable to estimate the impacts on providers in more detail” (91 FR 30453). [E]

The $488 billion is a best case, since states fund part of the state share from general revenue and step 2’s $163.7 billion may already count part of what providers stop paying; CMS’s data support no closer estimate. [I] CMS’s low-savings and high-savings estimates, paired by THEIA Services, widen the range to about $122 billion (low-savings, after step 3) at one end and about $1,126 billion (high-savings, after step 2) at the other, in 2026 dollars over 2026–2035 (Appendix D). [I]

CMS’s figures cover all providers, with no separate hospital figure; for the SDP rule CMS says it cannot attribute the impacts by provider type (91 FR 30462), and for the provider tax rule it expects the largest decreases to fall on hospitals (91 FR 46591). [E] Hospitals are projected to pay $61.8 billion of the $98.6 billion in provider taxes in calendar year 2026 (Table 4, 91 FR 46589) [E]; if that one-year share held for the decade, hospitals would also receive most of step 2’s $163.7 billion in tax relief, an allocation that is THEIA Services’ assumption, not CMS’s. [I]

## KFF’s $155 billion

KFF’s $155 billion, from its August 14, 2026 analysis of SDPs in 37 states, is the part of the SDP rule’s $510 billion federal cut that KFF reports is also counted in the provider tax rule. [E] That matches CMS’s $154.9 billion federal share of the $241.9 billion overlap (91 FR 46592). [I] KFF and CMS therefore report one finding, federal only versus federal plus state. [I]

## HFMA’s $56.6 billion

HFMA’s $56.6 billion appears, on THEIA Services’ reading, to subtract the $163.7 billion in lower provider taxes twice. [I] HFMA’s July 22, 2026 article, which describes the same no-OBBBA comparison point as CMS, reported that provider payments would fall $220.3 billion over 10 years and providers would save $163.7 billion in taxes, “leaving a net decrease of $56.6 billion.” [E] But $220.3 billion matches the provider line of CMS’s Table 12, which already subtracts the $163.7 billion [I]; THEIA Services found $56.6 billion in neither rule [I], and if this reading is right, HFMA understates the provider tax rule’s standalone net effect, a $220.3 billion loss. [I]

CMS’s wording may explain the reading. [I] CMS describes the $384.0 billion payment cut as shown in Table 12 (91 FR 46592), although the $384.0 billion appears in Table 11 and Table 12 shows the net −$220.3 billion. [E] Of the five comments on the provider tax rule that cite $220.3 billion (five distinct texts, the same as filed and corrected), four describe it as a payment cut (comments CMS-2026-2476-0049, -0085, -0190 and -0201) and one as net of lower taxes (-0212); none arrives at $56.6 billion. [E] Georgetown University’s Center for Children and Families reported the same $220.3 billion on July 27, 2026 as already net of providers’ lower taxes [E], and HFMA had not replied to THEIA Services’ request for confirmation, sent [DATE SENT], as of [DATE]. [I]

## POLITICO’s $681 billion and the Congressional Budget Office’s estimates

POLITICO’s $681 billion compares two estimates of the same OBBBA provisions on mismatched bases. [I] Its September 2, 2026 article reported that hospitals had expected to lose $340 billion through 2034 under the 2025 law, that the rules would produce about $756 billion in federal and $265 billion in state savings through 2035, and that, “as hospitals see it,” hospitals are losing $681 billion more than expected. [E]

The article names no source for the $340 billion. [I] The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimated that Section 71115 would reduce federal Medicaid outlays by $191.1 billion and Section 71116 by $149.4 billion over fiscal years 2025–2034, in dollars not adjusted for inflation (CBO, July 21, 2025, Table 7; October 28, 2025, pp. 4–5), and reports interactions among the OBBBA’s Medicaid provisions on separate lines (October 28, 2025, p. 6). [E] POLITICO’s $340 billion matches the sum of the two section figures, $340.5 billion, which CBO does not print. [I]

POLITICO’s CMS inputs mix scopes. [I] Its $756 billion, both rules’ federal savings measured alone ($510.1 billion + $245.8 billion, 2026–2035, 2026 dollars), leaves out the interaction between the two sections, as CBO’s section figures do on THEIA Services’ reading of CBO’s tables, but differs from them in years, dollars and baseline, and read as the two rules’ combined cut it counts the $154.9 billion federal overlap twice; its $265 billion is the SDP rule’s state spending reduction alone (sec. 3). [I] A gap between government savings estimates measures provider payments before tax relief, across all provider types, not a net hospital loss. [I]

On THEIA Services’ reading of CBO’s table layout, which CBO does not state in words, CBO’s section figures exclude the interaction between the sections; the CMS figure treated the same way is its federal total without the interaction, $755.9 billion for 2026–2035, while CMS’s $601.0 billion has no CBO counterpart (Appendix F). [I] Only CMS’s extra year lowers its figure: without 2035, the $601.0 billion becomes $501.5 billion and the no-interaction total $630.9 billion (federal, 2026–2034), nine years against CBO’s ten; removing the interaction, or converting 2026 dollars to CBO’s unadjusted dollars (CMS gives no conversion), raises it. [I] Year bases may also differ (sec. 3), and the direction of the remaining differences, what the provider tax rule adds to Section 71115 and the agencies’ comparison projections, is unknown, so the rest of the gap between these published estimates is unresolved. [I] THEIA Services found no independent derivation of $681 billion before September 2, 2026 [I]; one later comment restates it (comment CMS-2026-2476-0079; Appendix G). [E]

## ASPE’s $891 billion

ASPE’s $891 billion, from an August 12, 2026 report by HHS’s Office of the Assistant Secretary for Planning and Evaluation (ASPE), is a combined federal and state Medicaid spending cut from the OBBBA’s provider tax and SDP provisions over 2025–2034, used to estimate benefits to other payers (p. 11) [E]; ASPE does not say whether it removes the overlap between the two provisions. [I]

ASPE estimates up to 3.5 percent lower non-Medicaid prices [E], the counterpart of the $35.0 billion in tax relief CMS assigns to private payers [I], and $502 billion to $875 billion in benefits to non-Medicaid payers over 2025–2034 (pp. 10–11). [E] ASPE scored the OBBBA’s provisions directly rather than as CMS’s proposed rules would implement them, over different years, so its $891 billion cannot be compared directly with THEIA Services’ combined $916.9 billion. [I] Some organizations already net provider taxes against payments (Appendix F). [I]

# 7. Estimating one hospital’s position

A hospital can estimate its own position with the same three steps, because its own records hold the two figures public data lack: its SDP receipts and its provider tax and IGT payments. [I] State decisions remain the main uncertainty, so the result is a range for each year, not a single number. [I]

## Step 1. SDP revenue at risk

For each SDP program, the hospital adds its base managed care payment and its SDP for each service, compares the total with the cap (100 percent of Medicare in expansion states, 110 percent elsewhere), and treats the SDP amount above the cap as at risk. [I] Grandfathered programs fall by 10 percentage points of Medicare a year until they reach the cap, starting with the state’s first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028, while other programs get no phase-down (Section 71116; sec. 1). [E] The hospital’s own records and the state’s documents supply the inputs (Appendix C). [I] Step 1 covers only SDP amounts above the cap; it leaves out cuts that follow lost provider tax revenue, including the share of the provider tax rule’s cut that falls outside SDPs (at least 20 percent under CMS’s 80 percent ceiling; sec. 2), which a hospital should treat as a separate exposure. [I]

## Step 2. Lower provider taxes

Step 2 is the drop in the hospital’s own provider tax bill. [I] It is zero in non-expansion states unless the state enacted or raised the tax after July 4, 2025 [I], since CMS says taxes not in place on that date could not continue past October 1, 2026 (91 FR 46589) [E], and it is zero for a tax already at or below the threshold. [I] In expansion states, the hospital estimates its bill at each step of the lower threshold, 5.5 percent in federal fiscal year 2028 down to 3.5 percent from 2032, assuming the state lowers the tax to comply; the threshold limits a tax class’s revenue as a share of the class’s net patient revenue, not the hospital’s own rate, so the state’s plan for the tax decides the bill. [I] CMS intends to calculate and provide to states the new threshold percentage for each permissible class no later than September 30, 2028 (91 FR 46574) [E]; until then the state’s current tax is the starting point. [I] Step 2 is not directly comparable with the national $163.7 billion over 2026–2035, which is measured against a projection without the OBBBA, comes from $198.7 billion in lower collections that include $51.0 billion of tax increases avoided under the freeze, and is what remains after the $35.0 billion CMS assigns to private payers (sec. 3). [I]

## Step 3. Less money paid to fund SDPs

Step 3 is the money the hospital might stop paying to fund the state share of SDPs. [I] That money flows through provider taxes or through IGTs a public hospital or its owning local government makes. [I] It depends on a decision the state has not made, so the hospital models three cases: [I]

-   The state lowers the tax or transfer as SDPs shrink, maximizing the hospital’s step 3 saving.

-   The state keeps collecting the tax for other Medicaid costs, leaving no step 3 saving.

-   The state replaces part of the lost SDPs with general revenue or other payment programs, shrinking the step 1 loss. CMS’s 30 percent offset covers only the provider tax rule’s cuts (sec. 3): replacing those cuts beyond 30 percent departs from CMS’s assumption, while replacing SDP payments lost to the cap is relief the national range does not include. [I]

Step 3 must not count a tax cut already counted in step 2. [I] A state’s preprint, the form it submits to get CMS’s written prior approval for an SDP, lists funding sources by type and reports IGT dollars by transferring entity but no provider tax dollars (SDP preprint, Section VI) [E], and a state hospital association that works with the state on its provider tax program may hold member-level models of the tax and its payment pool. [I]

## Questions to ask the state Medicaid agency

-   Which SDP programs does the state treat as grandfathered, when does its first managed care rating period on or after January 1, 2028 begin, and how will it calculate each program’s Medicare-rate cap?

-   Which provider taxes and IGTs fund each program, and in what amounts?

-   Will the state lower provider tax rates as SDPs shrink, keep collecting the taxes for other Medicaid costs, or replace lost SDPs from other sources?

-   Will the state redesign or resubmit any program before the cap applies?

The three steps cover only the two rules, leaving out managed care contracting, commercial price changes and the OBBBA’s other Medicaid provisions, including eligibility changes that affect how many Medicaid patients a hospital treats. [I]

# 8. Services already under strain

Obstetric, pediatric and inpatient psychiatric services were shrinking or shifting before either rule was proposed (studies below) [E], and the rules’ effect on them cannot be predicted from public data because it depends on state budgets, managed care contracts and each hospital’s finances. [I] THEIA Services makes no prediction about closures or service cuts.

-   **Obstetrics.** GAO reported research showing that the number of rural hospitals providing obstetric services fell from 2004 through 2018, and that by 2018 more than half of rural counties had no hospital offering them (GAO-23-105515, October 2022). [E]

-   **Pediatrics.** A national study found that pediatric inpatient units fell 29.9 percent and pediatric beds 19.5 percent from 2008 to 2022, against a 4.4 percent decline in adult units (Michelson et al., December 2024). [E]

*[Figure 7] Pediatric inpatient units fell faster than adult units from 2008 to 2022, in one national study.*

-   **Inpatient psychiatry.** Psychiatric beds per 100,000 people fell from 11.3 to 9.06 in short-term acute care hospitals and rose from 16.8 to 19.5 in freestanding psychiatric hospitals between 2011 and 2023, while the total stayed about flat at 28.1 and 28.4 (Lindenfeld et al., PLoS Medicine, July 2025), with no statistically significant trend for any type of bed. [E]

The direction of the psychiatric estimates matters for adult Medicaid patients, because most freestanding psychiatric hospitals are institutions for mental diseases (IMDs; Appendix B), where federal Medicaid payment for adults aged 21 to 64 is restricted, with exceptions for managed care and certain demonstrations [I]; the restriction long predates both rules (Social Security Act § 1905(a)). [E] Where hospitals run obstetric, pediatric or psychiatric services below cost and cover the loss from other revenue, lower SDPs are a plausible pressure point. [C]

*[Figure 8] Psychiatric beds per 100,000 people by hospital type, 2011–2023. The combined total was about flat, and the authors found no statistically significant trend for any type of bed.*

## Rural hospitals have more time, not immunity

The OBBBA gives rural hospitals, as it defines them (Appendix B), until July 4, 2025, rather than May 1, for SDPs approved or sought through a documented good-faith effort, but their grandfathered payments still phase down from 2028 like other hospitals’ (Section 71116; sec. 1). [E]

Critical access, freestanding children’s and certain cancer hospitals are paid on cost and have no published Medicare rate, so their cap defaults to the state Medicaid plan rate (91 FR 30450) [E], which may leave little or no room for SDPs above base rates. [I] Virginia’s Medicaid agency urged CMS not to default payment limits to state plan rates for services without a Medicare equivalent (comment CMS-2026-1916-0755, p. 3). [E] THEIA Services does not assess whether rural hospitals are less able to absorb these changes; the studies cited track services, not hospital finances. [I]

# 9. What to watch

Each event below would change CMS’s estimates, change the rules, or show who bears the cuts. [I]

## Events that would change CMS’s estimates or the rules

1.  A final rule with a revised Regulatory Impact Analysis, especially any change to CMS’s 65 percent tax-financed assumption, which sets the size of the overlap between the rules, or to its assumed price ratios. [I]

2.  CMS’s analysis of how the two rules interact, which the SDP rule defers to future rulemaking (91 FR 30453) [E] and the provider tax rule partly supplies. [I]

3.  Either rule’s arrival for White House review, shown on reginfo.gov [E], which signals that a final rule is near. [I]

4.  Publication of CMS-2450, a separate proposed rule under White House review since May 12, 2026, whose agenda entry states an intent to rescind or revise parts of CMS’s 2024 managed care rule, including SDP requirements. [E]

5.  CMS approvals or renewals of individual SDPs under current rules, which can bring financing effects forward before either rule is final: in September 2026, one state’s SDP programs lapsed at the start of its fiscal year during a dispute over its provider tax calculation, and CMS authorized them about two and a half weeks later (Texas Tribune, September 17, 2026; Fierce Healthcare, September 18, 2026). [E]

6.  A decision on appeal in Texas v. CMS, 805 F. Supp. 3d 734 (E.D. Tex. 2025), which the Children’s Hospital Association of Texas describes as a permanent injunction against CMS’s hold harmless test for Texas’s local provider participation funds (comment CMS-2026-2476-0114, pp. 13–14). [E] An unverified research note gives the appeal as Fifth Circuit No. 25-40766, pending as of June 24, 2026. [I]

7.  Lawsuits or congressional action: as of September 22, 2026, THEIA Services located no lawsuit challenging either rule [I], and two July 2025 bills to repeal the underlying provisions had not advanced beyond committee referral (Appendix I). [E]

## Events that would show who bears the cuts

8.  Interim provider tax data states would file with CMS under the proposed rule for the quarter ending December 31, 2026, the first basis for calculating a state’s lower threshold (91 FR 46583) [I], ahead of final data due June 30, 2028 and CMS’s intention to calculate and provide to states the new threshold percentage for each permissible class no later than September 30, 2028 (91 FR 46574). [E]

9.  Any published analysis linking SDPs to the hospitals that receive them; THEIA Services found no public source that makes that link. [I]

10. Any multi-state estimate that subtracts lower provider taxes from payment cuts for both rules over the same years; at least one state hospital association has published paired gross and net figures (Appendix F) [E], but THEIA Services found no multi-state version. [I]

11. State legislative and budget decisions on the lower threshold, which decide whether states absorb the cuts, replace the lost money or pass the cuts on to providers. [C]
